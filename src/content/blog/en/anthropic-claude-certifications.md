Anthropic offers five Claude certifications, CCAO-F, CCDV-F, CCDV-P, CCAR-F and CCAR-P, priced from $99 to $175 and proctored by Pearson VUE. They are Anthropic's official professional credentials for Claude. The first, Claude Certified Architect - Foundations, launched in March 2026. Three more followed in July, and a fifth, for senior developers, arrived in November. Three of the five switch to new exam content on December 7. All five earn a Credly digital badge and stay valid for 12 months. Each has an official Exam Guide that lists the exam's domains and what share of the questions each domain carries. None of them is the same thing as the free Claude course certificates that circulate on social media. This article covers which one to take, what each one tests, and how they differ from the free certificates.

## Model vendors have started issuing their own credentials

Until recently, the best-known proctored AI certifications came from the cloud vendors: Microsoft's Azure AI certifications, Google Cloud's [Generative AI Leader](https://cloud.google.com/learn/certification/generative-ai-leader), [AWS Certified AI Practitioner](https://aws.amazon.com/certification/certified-ai-practitioner/). They test how to use AI services on that vendor's platform, and they cost roughly $99 to $200.

By 2026 a second kind had taken shape: the companies that build the models issuing credentials themselves. The three main model vendors are at very different stages:

Two differences are worth spelling out. What most people can get from OpenAI today [proves you finished a course](https://openai.com/index/openai-certificate-courses/), not that you passed an exam. Google does run [proctored exams](https://cloud.google.com/learn/certification/generative-ai-leader), but its cloud division issues them, and they test using AI on Google Cloud, not the Gemini model ecosystem itself.

So far, only one model vendor runs the whole system under its own model brand, with its own exam questions, third-party proctoring and a formal way to register: Anthropic, the enterprise-focused company behind Claude.

In March 2026, Anthropic launched its first credential, Claude Certified Architect - Foundations, together with the Claude Partner Network. The [announcement](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-partner-network) included a line few people noticed: *"Later this year, we'll introduce additional certifications for sellers, architects, and developers."* Four months later, part of that "later" arrived: one credential became four. A fifth followed in November.

## Which one should I take? Match the official recommended experience

Pick by the recommended experience in each Exam Guide: CCAO-F if you use Claude at work without building systems, CCAR-P if you have 3+ years of systems architecture or platform engineering, CCAR-F if you mainly design Claude systems, CCDV-F if you mainly write code that integrates Claude, and CCDV-P if you also run those systems in production. That part of each guide describes what the candidate Anthropic expects to just pass does day to day, and for how long. Matching your own work against it is more accurate than going by the credential's name.

The hardest pair to tell apart is CCDV-F and CCAR-F, since both test agents and tools. CCDV-F tests the skill of writing Claude into code: its guide recommends Python or TypeScript, and its heaviest domain is Applications and Integration. CCAR-F tests architectural judgment and trade-offs. Its guide states that it does not test implementation details of any specific programming language or framework, apart from what configuring tools and schemas requires. Its heaviest domain is agent architecture, and [Claude Code](/en/blog/claude-code-tutorial/) configuration has a domain of its own at 20%.

Developers have a senior credential too, CCDV-P. What separates it from CCDV-F is how far the work goes: CCDV-F tests building an application and shipping it, and CCDV-P tests running it after launch. Its guide assumes you've deployed at least one tool-using agent in production.

Once you've picked one, note that registration goes through an Anthropic partner organization; a personal account can't register. If your company isn't a partner, see the last section.

## Free Claude certificates vs. the five paid credentials

The free Claude certificates shared on social media are course-completion certificates, not proctored certifications.

They come from Anthropic's official [course catalog](https://anthropic.skilljar.com/), which has more than twenty free courses, from Claude Code in Action, Introduction to Model Context Protocol and Building with the Claude API to the AI Fluency series for non-technical roles. Finish one and you get a certificate that records you completed the course, with no proctoring, no common passing standard and no Credly badge.

The five Claude certifications in this article (CCAO-F, CCDV-F, CCDV-P, CCAR-F, CCAR-P) are standardized exams proctored by Pearson VUE and scored on one global scale.

Social media often treats the two as the same. When you see a headline about an "Anthropic certificate", check which kind it means.

## Are CCAF, CCA-F and CCAR-F the same Claude Certified Architect credential?

If you have searched for the Architect - Foundations credential before, you have probably met three different abbreviations. They aren't typos. Two date from the credential's first months, one used by Anthropic and the other by most community posts and third-party write-ups; the third is the formal exam code added in July.

When Anthropic launched the credential in March 2026, the announcement used only the full name, with no formal abbreviation. Anthropic's own follow-up communications, including an official email and the short URL [clau.de/CCAF](https://clau.de/CCAF), used **CCAF**. Community posts and most third-party write-ups used **CCA-F**. You may also see it called "Anthropic Certified Architect"; it's the same exam.

In July 2026, when the program expanded to four credentials and the exams moved to Pearson VUE, Architect - Foundations got its formal exam code: **CCAR-F**. The added R appears to be there to distinguish it from the Associate credential launched at the same time, CCAO-F.

All three abbreviations name the same credential at different times. For anything external-facing, such as a LinkedIn certification entry or a résumé, the current official code CCAR-F is the most precise.

## Why five credentials

The five credentials are cut by role: one for people who don't code, two for developers and two for architects, with each of those pairs split into Foundations and Professional. When Anthropic released the first four in July, its [announcement](https://claude.com/blog/four-role-based-claude-certifications) called them "role-based certifications." Partner Academy's [certifications page](https://anthropic-partners.skilljar.com/page/partner-certifications) now writes the split down: it groups the five under three roles, Associate, Developer and Architect, and says to start at Foundations and move up to Professional where one exists.

The one most worth noticing is CCAO-F (Associate), for non-technical roles. A PM who evaluates output quality, an operations lead who improves processes, an HR manager who plans internal training: Anthropic set an exam for them too, which counts these people among those an [AI adoption](/en/blog/enterprise-ai-adoption-bottleneck/) has to train.

In its March [announcement](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-partner-network), Anthropic said certifications for sellers, architects and developers would come later in the year. As of November 18, the certifications page lists five, and the new one is CCDV-P, the senior Developer credential. The same page also describes Associate as the credential for consultants, sellers and delivery leads, and notes that CCAO-F doesn't count toward Claude Partner Network tier eligibility.

## Rules shared by all five

All five credentials share one set of rules: Pearson VUE proctoring online or at a test center, a passing score of 720 on a 100–1,000 scale, a Credly badge valid for 12 months, free renewal before it expires, and waiting periods between retakes that grow from 14 to 30 to 90 days. The details below come from the [Anthropic Partner Academy FAQ](https://anthropic-partners.skilljar.com/page/faq-certifications) (the primary source) and each credential's Exam Guide:

**After you pass: renewal.** A credential is valid for 12 months. Before it expires, you can renew for free with a non-proctored online assessment on Partner Academy, without another Pearson VUE exam. If it lapses, you pay the full fee and retake the whole exam. Every guide states one exception: when exam content changes substantially, Anthropic may require holders to retake the full exam instead of the renewal assessment.

**If you fail: waiting periods.** After a first failed attempt you wait 14 days, after a second 30 days, and after a third 90 days. You can sit the same exam at most four times within 12 months, and each attempt costs the full fee again. The limits count per exam, so failing one doesn't stop you from registering for another.

A few more rules decide whether you get into the exam and whether you get your fee back. On exam day you need a valid, government-issued photo ID; if its name doesn't match your registration, you can't test that day and the fee isn't refunded. To change the name on your registration, use Update Exam Profile in your Partner Academy profile. You can do this once a year, and your name locks once you confirm. If you've already used that yearly change and still need a correction, request it from Anthropic Certification Support. Missing a scheduled exam also forfeits the fee.

## How to read the official Exam Guides

If you're preparing seriously, read the official Exam Guides before any third-party prep material. The PDFs download without a login. Direct links are in the table above, and the [Partner Academy certifications page](https://anthropic-partners.skilljar.com/page/partner-certifications) lists them too.

The guides share a structure. Every exam is 120 minutes of multiple-choice questions, and each question states how many answers to pick. Start with three parts:

- Domain weights: what share of the questions each domain carries.
- Sample questions: answers and explanations included, so you can see how questions are written and how hard they are.
- The score report description: besides pass or fail and your score, the report lists your percentage correct in each domain, so a candidate who fails can see what to work on.

The heaviest domain is Production Integration, Reliability, and Knowledge Grounding, at 20%. Across the domains, the guide lists problems you only meet after launch, for example:

- When a call fails, the fallback (the backup path the request takes instead) has to tell apart three stop reasons and handle each differently: rate limits, safety refusals, and running out of context window.
- CI/CD (the automated test-and-deploy pipeline) gets evaluation gates that block bad releases, and an LLM-as-judge is calibrated against human labels, then rechecked after model updates so its scoring doesn't drift.
- Several agents work in parallel, each in its own worktree (a separate working copy of the same code repository), and AI code review first runs in shadow mode, where it records findings but blocks nothing.

The CCDV-P guide states no number of years, while CCDV-F, CCAR-F and CCAR-P all say how much experience they expect. Instead it describes who the exam is for: senior and staff engineers, platform engineers and site reliability engineers (SREs), and solutions engineers responsible for live Claude applications. The agent a candidate has deployed should use tools, not be just an LLM chatbot or summarizer.

There's no prerequisite. CCDV-F-level knowledge is recommended but not required, and the guide treats the CCDV-F scope as assumed knowledge. For preparation, it suggests operating at least one tool-using Claude agent in production. That system should trace each model call and tool call end to end (distributed tracing) and track cost. It should also have SLIs and SLOs: the measures of service quality you track, and the target for each. On top of that come an evaluation suite that gates releases, layered guardrails, and a practice of incident response and postmortems.

It also covers choosing the agent runtime, meaning what actually runs the agent's loop: your own Messages API loop, the Agent SDK or headless Claude Code (Claude Code run by a script, with no one at the keyboard) on your own servers, or Claude Managed Agents.

The two added domains are Governance, Safety, and Risk Management and Stakeholder Communication and Lifecycle Management. They cover where guardrails go and which architectural controls satisfy a compliance obligation. They also ask you to spot commitments in discovery notes or a draft SLA (the service-level agreement promised to the client) that the architecture can't meet, and to check whether an architecture decision record (the document recording why a design was chosen) spells out its trade-offs.

RAG (retrieval-augmented generation: fetching relevant documents and handing them to the model) sits in the Integration domain. Given the documents, the query pattern, and the latency and cost limits, you choose among a pre-built RAG pipeline, agentic search with tools, and putting the full document set in context. CCAR-P tests something closer to an architect who owns the whole solution: beyond how the system is built, choosing the deployment platform and designing governance and risk controls into it.

The new guide's recommended experience is 3+ years of systems architecture or platform engineering plus at least 6 months using Claude in production. The July version said "Claude or a comparable LLM." There's no prerequisite. CCAR-F-level knowledge is recommended but not required, and the guide treats the CCAR-F scope as assumed knowledge.

For preparation, the guide recommends designing and delivering at least one end-to-end Claude solution in production, covering model and deployment-platform selection, retrieval or tool integration, an evaluation suite, observability and layered guardrails. It also suggests practicing on other people's architecture diagrams, configuration files, logs and traces, evaluation results and code, because exam questions hand you these directly.
